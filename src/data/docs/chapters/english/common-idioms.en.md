# Common Idioms — Everyday English Idioms

Imagine someone says to you "break a leg!" — do you think they're telling you to actually break your leg? 😂 No! The real meaning is "good luck!" These kinds of expressions are called **idioms** — where the words don't mean what they literally say; the whole phrase has a separate meaning.

English has hundreds of idioms. Native speakers use them every day — in conversation, in movies, in songs. If you don't know idioms, you'll miss a lot. In this chapter, we'll learn 30+ of the most common idioms — organized in 5 categories.

> [!tip] How to Recognize an Idiom
> If you can't figure out the meaning of a phrase from the individual words — it's probably an idiom. For example, "piece of cake" doesn't mean a slice of cake; it means "very easy."

> [!important] When NOT to Use Idioms
> Idioms are **informal** — great with friends, in chat, in casual talk. But don't use them in **formal writing** (academic papers, official emails, CVs). There, clear and simple language works better.

## 1. Daily Life — Everyday Life Idioms

These are the idioms you hear most in everyday life. With friends, with family — everywhere.

| Idiom | Literal Translation (Funny) | Real Meaning | Example |
|-------|------------------------------|--------------|---------|
| piece of cake | a slice of cake | very easy | The exam was a **piece of cake**. |
| break a leg! | break your leg! | good luck! (before a stage performance) | You have a presentation? **Break a leg!** |
| costs an arm and a leg | costs an arm and a leg | very expensive | That phone **costs an arm and a leg**. |
| hit the books | hit the books | to study hard | Exams are coming, time to **hit the books**! |
| under the weather | under the weather | feeling sick / unwell | I'm feeling **under the weather** today. |
| once in a blue moon | once in a blue moon | very rarely | We meet **once in a blue moon**. |
| spill the beans | spill the beans | to reveal a secret | Come on, **spill the beans**! What happened? |
| ring a bell | ring a bell | sounds familiar | That name **rings a bell**. |
| cut corners | cut corners | to take shortcuts / reduce quality | Don't **cut corners** on safety. |
| beat around the bush | beat around the bush | to avoid the main topic | Stop **beating around the bush**, tell me! |

```
Tania: How was yesterday's exam?
Arif:  Awesome! It was a piece of cake.
Tania: Really? I thought it went badly for me.
Arif:  Why? What happened?
Tania: I didn't study properly, I was feeling under the weather.
```

> [!tip] "Break a leg" — from the theater world
> This idiom comes from the theater. It was said to wish actors good luck. Now it can be said before any performance or presentation. It might sound strange — but it's very positive!

> [!note] "Spill the beans" — from ancient voting
> In ancient Greece, voting was done using white beans (yes) and black beans (no). If someone knocked over the jar, the beans would spill and the result would be revealed early. That's where "spill the beans" = to reveal a secret comes from.

## 2. Work / Office — Workplace Idioms

These idioms are very useful in the office, in meetings, and with colleagues. You'll hear them often in professional conversations.

| Idiom | Literal Translation (Funny) | Real Meaning | Example |
|-------|------------------------------|--------------|---------|
| on the same page | on the same page | in agreement / on the same topic | Let's make sure we're **on the same page**. |
| think outside the box | think outside the box | to think creatively | We need to **think outside the box**. |
| touch base | touch base | to connect / get an update | Let's **touch base** next week. |
| ballpark figure | ballpark figure | an approximate estimate | Can you give me a **ballpark figure**? |
| back to square one | back to square one | to start over from the beginning | The plan failed, **back to square one**. |
| learn the ropes | learn the ropes | to learn the basics / get trained | New job? You'll **learn the ropes** soon. |
| bring to the table | bring to the table | to contribute | What skills do you **bring to the table**? |
| get the ball rolling | get the ball rolling | to get started | Let's **get the ball rolling** on this project. |
| in the loop | in the loop | kept informed | Keep me **in the loop** about updates. |
| call the shots | call the shots | to be in charge / make decisions | Who **calls the shots** here? |

```
Manager: We need to think outside the box for this campaign.
Rana:   Sure! I have some ideas.
Manager: Great. Let's keep everyone in the loop.
Rana:   Perfect. Let's get the ball rolling!
```

> [!tip] Which idioms to use in the office?
> "On the same page," "touch base," and "in the loop" — these three are the safest. You can use them in meetings or emails. But avoid them in formal reports.

## 3. Feelings — Emotional Idioms

Emotions, anger, joy — idioms are great for expressing all of them.

| Idiom | Literal Translation (Funny) | Real Meaning | Example |
|-------|------------------------------|--------------|---------|
| over the moon | over the moon | extremely happy | She was **over the moon** with the news! |
| see red | see red | very angry | When he lied, I **saw red**. |
| under a cloud | under a cloud | under suspicion / feeling down | He left the company **under a cloud**. |
| a bundle of nerves | a bundle of nerves | very nervous / tense | Before the interview, I was **a bundle of nerves**. |
| on cloud nine | on cloud nine | extremely happy / satisfied | Got the job! I'm **on cloud nine**! |
| hot under the collar | hot under the collar | embarrassed / angry | The criticism made him **hot under the collar**. |
| down in the dumps | down in the dumps | very sad | After the breakup, she was **down in the dumps**. |
| tickled pink | tickled pink | very happy / delighted | Your gift **tickled me pink**! |
| heart of gold | heart of gold | very kind person | My grandmother has a **heart of gold**. |
| cry over spilled milk | cry over spilled milk | to worry about the past | Don't **cry over spilled milk**, move on. |

```
Sadia: You got the job — how do you feel?
Rima:  I'm over the moon! I can't believe it.
Sadia: I'm so happy for you! We need to celebrate.
Rima:  Yes! You'll come, right?
Sadia: Of course! Where are we going?
Rima:  Let's decide today.
```

> [!note] "Over the moon" vs "On cloud nine"
> Both mean extremely happy. "Over the moon" is a bit more common, while "On cloud nine" is slightly more poetic. Both are positive and enthusiastic.

> [!tip] "Cry over spilled milk" — a valuable lesson
> This isn't just an idiom; it's a life lesson. There's no benefit in being sad about what's already happened. Look forward. Remembering this idiom can boost your mental peace!

## 4. Success / Failure — Idioms for Success and Failure

Good exam results, getting a job, a promotion — these idioms are very useful in all such contexts.

| Idiom | Literal Translation (Funny) | Real Meaning | Example |
|-------|------------------------------|--------------|---------|
| hit the nail on the head | hit the nail on the head | exactly right / spot on | You **hit the nail on the head** with that analysis. |
| on top of the world | on top of the world | extremely successful / satisfied | After winning the award, she felt **on top of the world**. |
| go the extra mile | go the extra mile | to make extra effort | He always **goes the extra mile** for clients. |
| by the skin of one's teeth | by the skin of one's teeth | barely passing / narrowly | I passed the exam **by the skin of my teeth**. |
| bite off more than you can chew | bite off more than you can chew | to take on more than you can handle | Don't **bite off more than you can chew**. |
| the sky is the limit | the sky is the limit | there's no limit | With hard work, **the sky is the limit**! |
| come out on top | come out on top | to win / succeed | Despite challenges, she **came out on top**. |
| fall flat | fall flat | to fail / not work | The joke **fell flat** — nobody laughed. |
| bite the dust | bite the dust | to fail / come to an end | My old laptop finally **bit the dust**. |
| pick yourself up | pick yourself up | to recover / start again | You failed? **Pick yourself up** and try again. |

```
Father: How are the results?
Son:   First place! I'm on top of the world!
Father: Fantastic! The sky is the limit for you.
Son:   Thanks, Dad. I'll do even better.
Father: Yes, go the extra mile. Success will come.
```

> [!tip] "Go the extra mile" — great for the office!
> This idiom is very valuable in professional life. The person who "goes the extra mile" — meaning does more than average — gets the promotion, becomes the boss's favorite. It applies not just to work, but to life in general.

## 5. Time — Idioms About Time

Talking about time is the most common thing. Here are some useful time idioms.

| Idiom | Literal Translation (Funny) | Real Meaning | Example |
|-------|------------------------------|--------------|---------|
| time flies | time flies | time passes quickly | **Time flies** when you're having fun! |
| in the nick of time | in the nick of time | just in time / at the last moment | I arrived **in the nick of time**. |
| kill time | kill time | to pass time | I read books to **kill time**. |
| time is money | time is money | time is valuable | Hurry up, **time is money**! |
| on the dot | on the dot | exactly on time | The meeting starts at 9 **on the dot**. |
| around the clock | around the clock | 24 hours / nonstop | The hospital works **around the clock**. |
| in no time | in no time | very quickly | I'll finish this **in no time**. |
| behind the times | behind the times | old-fashioned / outdated | My grandfather is a bit **behind the times**. |
| once upon a time | once upon a time | long ago (story opening) | **Once upon a time**, there was a king... |
| against the clock | against the clock | racing against time | We're working **against the clock** to finish. |

```
Rakib: I'm running late! What should I do?
Nila:  Hurry up! Time is money!
Rakib: I know, but I'm stuck in traffic.
Nila:  Somehow, get there in the nick of time.
Rakib: I'm trying!
```

> [!tip] "Time flies" — very common
> This is the most common time idiom. When you meet an old friend, when a year passes — you can always say: "Time flies!" It means "so much time has passed already!"

## Bonus: Confusing Idioms — Misunderstanding These Can Be Dangerous!

Some idioms, if taken literally, can create very embarrassing situations. Let's look at them.

| Idiom | Wrong Meaning (Funny) | Real Meaning | Warning |
|-------|------------------------|--------------|---------|
| pull someone's leg | pull someone's leg | to joke / tease someone | Don't think they're actually pulling a leg! |
| kick the bucket | kick the bucket | to die | informal, disrespectful — use with caution! |
| let the cat out of the bag | let the cat out of the bag | to reveal a secret | Nothing to do with a cat |
| when pigs fly | when pigs fly | never | Used to describe something impossible |
| barking up the wrong tree | barking up the wrong tree | pursuing the wrong direction | Nothing to do with a dog |

> [!important] "Kick the bucket" — be careful!
> This idiom means "to die" — it's very informal and a bit crude. Don't use it in front of someone or in a serious situation. It only works in casual conversation with very close friends.

## When to Use Idioms, and When Not To

> [!important] Use them — when:
> - In casual conversation with friends
> - On WhatsApp chat or social media
> - While watching movies or TV series to understand them
> - When they fit naturally in conversation

> [!important] Don't use them — when:
> - Writing an academic paper or thesis
> - In official emails or formal reports
> - In a job interview (be careful, one or two might be okay)
> - With a non-native speaker who doesn't know them
> - In very serious situations (hospital, funeral)

> 1. **Visualize** — when you hear "piece of cake," picture a cake in your mind, then connect it to "easy"

> 3. **Study in groups** — by category (feelings, work, time)
> 4. **Use them** — in chat, in conversation, wherever you can
